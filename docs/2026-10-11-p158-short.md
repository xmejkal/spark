## What the design assumes from your words

Your words of 2026-10-10 are quoted in the design's §0. Three readings there are mine, and you can strike any:

- **"Always simulates" is a target, not a promise.** A board whose chip Wokwi does not simulate cannot be simulated by
  any default; spark says so, as a stated limit, instead of guessing.
- **"Tolerates problems" means placeholders and stated limits, not findings.** A fault found in a design still answers
  `problems`, exit 1. If findings could ride along an `ok`, exit 0 would mean nothing.
- **"A general board" is a library board chosen for its chip and marked as standing in** for the one you own, so every
  later verdict carries that mark until a real record replaces it.

## The architecture in one paragraph

Not a rewrite. Seven units, four of them new and small, under one rule: a mistake lands in one named place with one
named reason. `outcomes` (exists) is the one answer shape every command prints, in text and JSON alike. `where` (new)
is the one place that knows which folder is spark, which is the project and which is the store, and the one guard on
every write into spark's own folder. `ledger` (new) holds the assumptions in force: every placeholder, every stated
limit, every unverified fact, so a placeholder may flow through the chain but can never disappear from the final
verdict. `store` stays, with a small change. `state` (new) records what ran, on what, and what is stale, so a changed
fact reruns only its downstream. The record contracts (boards first) become one table each, read by the validator, the
docs and the editor alike. `cli` (new) is the one `main` every script runs through, so one exit contract holds
everywhere. The chain becomes a build graph over these; the MCP face and the eval suite come last, as settings and
tests over the same answer shape, not as a second product.

## How the order works

Two lanes, alternating. **The layer lane** carries the design's steps in the design's order (I0 → I5). **The fix lane**
carries part 1's 72 ranked changes in part 1's order, silent harm first, each pulled when its hard prerequisites are
Done. A card is one concern, names its test and the failure message it will print, and says what it must not break in
the three projects. 27 of the 108 cards re-scope cards already on the board instead of filing new ones.

**The first slice, proposed for Ready:** C1 the dated baseline of the three projects, C2 the data pass over the drawer
(most entries carry no `function`, so no need can reach them; needs you), C3 the mutation sweep made honest, C4 a board
whose simulator stand-in is another chip is refused, C5 the bin's `make check` pinned against spark changes, C6 an
interpreter built for another chip is refused, C7 the crash-on-bad-input invariant over every `main`.

## Decisions for you

The design lists twenty-one, each with options and a recommendation (§16). The ones the first slice rests on:

1. **DP1 · The base.** Recommended: the smallest-layer approach (A1) with grafts from the other two: the build graph's
   staleness and the data-first contracts. Not a rewrite.
2. **DP2 · The order.** Your boards-first wish against part 1's silent-harm rule. Recommended: measure first (I0's
   baseline and the data pass), then the two lanes alternate, so the first board card (C4) is the first fix card.
3. **DP3 · P100's round A as the baseline.** Recommended: run it on today's spark before any output-changing card
   merges, so every later change is measured against a recorded run.
4. **DP4 · Placeholders and the exit code.** Recommended: a run on placeholders may answer `ok`, exit 0, with the count
   in the verdict line; a named target (a Gerber, a flash) refuses while any placeholder it rests on is in force.
5. **PQ1 · Filing.** Recommended: in waves, the first slice first, not 71 cards at once.

The other sixteen can wait for their iteration. Two from part 1 stand too: how to count dependencies when ordering,
and whether an answer that leaves out a fact it read counts as silent.

## What this cost, and the lesson

Part 2 took 13 agents and about 45 million input-equivalent tokens, two thirds of it re-reading: the documents had no
length budget, every stage read every earlier stage whole, and each fold rewrote a whole file. The plan grew from
30,400 to 49,500 words in its one fold. The run was stopped before its second full round. From now on, every document
you read has a length budget and a short version like this one, agents get pointers instead of whole files, and
refuters edit instead of rewrite. That rule is in Claude's working memory today; putting it on the process page's token
budget is a chore for the board.
